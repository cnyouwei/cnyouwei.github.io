1. Introduction

In modern Industrial Engineering practice, the central design challenge is to “optimize the system.” A decision maker must:

  • allocate limited resources
  • while balancing competing objectives.
IEDA3010 translates real decision problems into optimization models—identify objectives and constraints, formulate linear/network/integer/dynamic programs, and solve for optimal policy.

A typical constraint we see in IEDA3010 is a budget constraint

.

  • Interpretation: producing quantity

    incurs unit cost

    ; the total spend across all decisions cannot exceed the budget

    .

In practice, unit costs

are not fixed.

  • They can vary with spot prices, surge incentives, supplier lead times, and congestion.

  • A deterministic budget constraint

    is often only an approximation; the true spend is random and may violate

    unless we plan for risk.

Stochastic Models extends the prescriptive workflow to environments where the inputs and system evolution are random:

  • demand fluctuates, service times vary, failures occur, and travel times are uncertain.

As a result, the “right” decision is no longer optimized against a single deterministic scenario; it is optimized against risk, service levels, and long-run performance.

1.1. Where Randomness Enters Engineered Systems

1.2. Decisions under Uncertainty: Why Stochastic Models Matter

Across systems in Example 0.1, Example 0.2 and Example 0.3 , the decision maker must act before the randomness unfolds:

  • capacity is provisioned, fleets are positioned, and inventory is allocated days or weeks ahead.

Planning based on a “average” is fragile, operational performance is driven by variability:

  • Demand arrives in bursts, not as a smooth flow.
  • A small fraction of jobs take much longer than the mean.
  • Rare disruptions (failures, congestion, weather) dominate tail risk.

1.2.1. Case study: Waiting-Time Paradox

🙌 Raise your hand if this has happened to you.

Consider a simplified scanario where buses arrive exactly every 20 minutes. What is your expected waiting time until the next bus arrives?

How: You are not sampling bus gaps uniformly—you are sampling time.

  • Long gaps occupy more time on the clock, so you are more likely to arrive during a long gap than a short one.
  • As a result, the gap you “observe” tends to be longer than a typical gap between buses.

Inspection paradox: when interarrival times are variable, random inspections (your arrival time) are biased toward seeing longer intervals.

When: Passengers experience longer waits whenever interarrival times are irregular.

Suppose buses come “every 20 minutes”, the mean experienced waiting time depends on how variable the arrivals are:

  • always larger than 10 = 20/2 minutes;
  • equals 10 minutes only when interarrival times are perfectly deterministic;
  • equals 20 minutes (!) for a realistic random bus arrival model.

More on this in renewal process.

1.2.2. Case Study: Phantom Traffic Jam

🙌 Raise your hand if this has happened to you.

  • When traffic is dense, tiny random disturbances (one driver hesitates, taps the brake, or leaves a slightly larger gap) get amplified.
  • The next driver reacts a bit later and brakes a bit harder; the next one does the same. - Within seconds, a stop-and-go wave forms and travels backward through the stream.

Why: The system breaks because it is operating near a critical point: there is not enough slack to absorb variability. A small fluctuation does not die out; it cascades into a macroscopic jam that persists long after the original disturbance is gone.

A highway designed for average traffic flow will inevitably operate near capacity during peak hours. In that regime, small random fluctuations in driving behavior (hesitation, braking, lane changes) amplify into phantom jams.

Possible solution: Reduce variability. Automated vehicles smooth accelerations and shorten reaction lags, damping disturbances. (Why subway systems rarely “jam”: they run on centralized control, scheduled interarrival times, and limited interaction variability.)

When system utilization is high, small variability is not “noise”—it becomes the driver of performance, which is why we need stochastic models. (More on this in queueing theory.)

1.2.3. Case Study: Pop Mart Blind-Box

🙌 Raise your hand if you have ever tried to get a “drop” item that sold out.

Suppose last month you sold 10,000 boxes on average per drop. For the next drop, should you simply produce 10,000 again?

Two drops can have the same average demand but feel totally different:

  • Stable world: demand is consistently near 10,000. Ordering “around 10,000” is usually fine.
  • Hype world: most drops are modest, but occasionally demand spikes overnight (celebrity post, viral unboxing).

  • What hurts more?

    • Selling out instantly (lost revenue, angry fans, bad UX).
    • Overstocking (dead inventory, weaker brand momentum).

Blind-box products create two layers of randomness:

  • demand uncertainty (hype, time-of-day spikes, influencer shocks), and
  • “mix” uncertainty (which variants customers chase, and how fast the rare ones get depleted).

Planning based on an average can be systematically wrong because what matters is the probability of extreme outcomes.

You must commit inventory before the demand spike reveals itself. Stochastic thinking forces you to plan for the distribution (how often a viral surge occurs), not just the mean.

More on this in IEDA4420 Dynamic Pricing and Revenue Management.

1.3. Stochastic Model: Basic Concepts

Any realistic model of a real-world phenomenon must account for randomness. In many engineered systems, the quantities we care about—demand, processing times, failures, travel times—are not predictable in advance, but exhibit inherent variation that materially affects performance.

Random variables are the building blocks of stochastic models:

  • each one encodes an uncertain quantity of interest (e.g., demand, service time, failures).

By connecting multiple random variables through the system’s operational logic (flows, routing, and orders), we obtain a time-evolving description of the system. In other words, uncertainty does not appear as isolated “noise”—it propagates through time and across components. Naturally, we organize a time-indexed family of random variables to describe this evolution. This object is a stochastic process.

1.4. Overview of Topics

  • Discrete-time stochastic processes (time in steps: days, jobs, decisions)

    • Discrete-time Markov chains (DTMCs): state transitions each step.

      • Gambler’s ruin; random walk with absorbing boundaries.

    • Martingales: “fair game” processes and what they imply about predictability.

      • Why no betting system beats a fair game (without edge).

  • Continuous-time stochastic processes (time on the clock: seconds, minutes, hours)

    • Counting / renewal processes: modeling event arrivals over time.

      • Customer orders, calls, ride requests.

    • Continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs): random transition times between states.

      • Machine failure/repair (birth–death models).

  • Queueing systems (arrivals + service + congestion dynamics)

    • Hospital/clinic patient flow; call-center waits; platform latency under load.

  • Decision-making under uncertainty

    • Markov decision processes (MDPs): optimize when the system state is random and evolving.

      • Inventory control and replenishment under random demand.
